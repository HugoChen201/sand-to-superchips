Chapter 1

Sand → silicon

From beach to blank canvas — how sand becomes the most perfect manufactured object on Earth.

Complete
0FoundationWhy silicon at all

Sand is mostly quartz, which is silicon dioxide, and silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust. It's a natural semiconductor, meaning its electrons can be coaxed into conducting on command. Silicon's band gap is 1.12 eV, compared with germanium's 0.66 eV; that wider gap means far less electrical leakage at high temperatures, so silicon devices stay predictable when the heat rises.

But the real killer feature is the skin it grows. Heat silicon in oxygen and it forms silicon dioxide (SiO₂), a near-perfect insulator that can be grown with atomic precision. That clean silicon–oxide interface made the modern MOSFET transistor possible. Germanium powered the first transistors in 1947, but its oxide is unstable and water-soluble, so silicon won and never looked back.

In plain English: silicon can be the road for electrons and grow its own perfectly fitted guardrail.

Why the oxide is the real moat

Heat silicon past ~900°C in oxygen or steam and oxygen molecules react with the surface: Si + O₂ → SiO₂ — glass. The elegant part: the oxide doesn't just pile on top, it consumes the silicon as it grows — about 44% of the final glass layer sits below where the original surface was. The boundary between crystal and glass ends up atomically clean, with almost no defects, which is why a MOSFET gate works: the gate electrode is insulated from the channel, but its electric field still reaches through the glass to switch the transistor.

One material does three jobs: it's a superb insulator (band gap ~9 eV), it can be grown rather than deposited (nothing else gives that pristine interface), and it's a perfect mask — dopants barely diffuse through it, and etchants attack silicon and oxide selectively, so oxide patterns literally define where transistors get built. Germanium's oxide (GeO₂) is water-soluble and falls apart at chip-processing temperatures — it would wash off in the fab. That's the deeper reason silicon won.

Two flavors: dry oxidation (pure O₂) is slow but pristine — gate oxides; wet oxidation (steam) is fast and thick — field oxides and masks. Growth follows the Deal-Grove model: fast at first, then slowing as oxygen must diffuse through the glass already grown.

The kicker: this perfect glass hit a wall — by the mid-2000s gate oxides were ~1.2 nanometers (five atomic layers) and electrons quantum-tunneled straight through, forcing the switch to hafnium-based high-k dielectrics at the 45nm node.

1ReductionMetallurgical-grade silicon

Quartz and carbon go into a giant submerged-arc furnace at around 2,000°C. The core reaction is SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO: oxygen leaves the quartz as carbon monoxide gas, while elemental silicon pools below. The carbon charge is a deliberate mix of coal, coke, and wood chips, chosen to keep the furnace porous enough for gases to escape.

Out comes silicon at 98–99% purity. Sounds pure; for chips it's filthy. This grade mostly goes to aluminum alloys and silicone rubber. The furnaces are enormously electricity-hungry, which is why plants cluster around reliable cheap power — Norway's hydropower is a classic example.

In plain English: at extreme heat, carbon grabs the oxygen from quartz and leaves crude silicon behind.

Companies behind this step

Metallurgical-grade silicon is made in huge arc furnaces by specialists including Elkem of Norway and Ferroglobe, whose plants span the United States, Spain, and France. Wacker Chemie also produces its own MG-silicon in Norway, feeding the company's downstream polysilicon plants and tightening its control over a critical raw input.

2PurificationPolysilicon via the Siemens process

This is where “nine nines” happens. Crude silicon reacts with hydrochloric acid to make trichlorosilane gas, which is distilled — a fantastic purifier — before entering a bell-jar Siemens reactor. Inside, purified gas flows over U-shaped silicon filament rods heated to roughly 1,100°C. The gas decomposes and deposits elemental silicon layer by layer until the hair-thin starter rods grow into thick, gleaming polysilicon rods.

The result is 99.9999999% pure silicon: fewer than one stray atom per billion. The process uses roughly 50–100 kWh of electricity per kilogram of silicon, while hydrochloric acid and other process gases circulate in a largely closed recycling loop. That appetite for power is one reason China now makes the large majority of the world's polysilicon.

In plain English: trichlorosilane is silicon in gas form, so it can be distilled clean, then plated back onto hot silicon rods one layer at a time.

Companies behind this step

The Western, Japanese, and Korean semiconductor-grade producers include Wacker Chemie (Burghausen, Germany and Charleston, Tennessee), Hemlock Semiconductor (Hemlock, Michigan — a Corning + Shin-Etsu joint venture), Tokuyama in Japan, and OCI in Korea and Malaysia.

REC Silicon in Moses Lake, Washington takes a different route: fluidized-bed-reactor technology creates granular polysilicon instead of the rods produced by the Siemens process.

Semiconductor-grade poly is only a small, ultra-demanding slice of output. The vast majority of polysilicon goes to solar panels, and more than 80% of all polysilicon is made in China by firms such as Tongwei, GCL, Daqo, and Xinte.

3Crystal growthThe Czochralski pull

Chunks of polysilicon are melted in a quartz crucible at 1,414°C. A small seed crystal touches the surface and is winched upward at just millimeters per minute while rotating; the crucible turns the opposite way to even out the heat. Argon flows through the chamber to sweep away contaminants. The melt freezes onto the seed as one continuous crystal lattice — a single crystal two meters long, hundreds of kilos, with zero grain boundaries.

The grower first pulls a pencil-thin “neck,” which filters crystal dislocations out of the seed, then widens the crystal into the full-diameter body. Boron or phosphorus is added directly to the melt to set the wafer's base electrical type before the crystal even exists. No grain boundaries means every transistor starts from the same orderly atomic grid. Fun fact: Jan Czochralski discovered the method in 1916 after accidentally dipping his pen into molten tin instead of his inkwell.

In plain English: Czochralski is pronounced “choh-KRAHL-skee” — slowly pulling one perfect crystal from a spinning melt.

Companies behind this step

The vessel holding molten silicon is a technical marvel too: an ultra-pure fused-quartz crucible. Much of the world's purest quartz comes from Spruce Pine, North Carolina, where it is mined by Sibelco and The Quartz Corp. When Hurricane Helene shut those mines in September 2024, the disruption rattled the chip supply chain because this grade of quartz has so few substitutes. The crystal-pulling furnaces are specialized systems built by equipment firms such as Germany's PVA TePla.

4FinishingFrom ingot to wafer

The ingot (called a boule) is sliced by diamond wire only about 100 microns thick. Even so, the kerf — silicon ground into sawdust by each cut — adds up across hundreds of slices. A finished 300mm wafer is roughly 775 microns thick, about the thickness of a sturdy credit card, yet its working surface must be flat and clean at the atomic scale.

Slicing is only the start. Edge grinding rounds the rim so it will not chip; lapping flattens both faces; chemical etching strips away saw damage; and chemical-mechanical polishing produces the final mirror. A small notch marks the crystal orientation so every fab tool aligns the lattice the same way. Not every wafer becomes chips: fabs sacrifice “test wafers” to calibrate deposition, etch, and cleaning tools before valuable production wafers enter.

In plain English: a boule is a giant silicon carrot, but turning its slices into fab-ready mirrors takes several precision finishing stages.

Companies behind this step

The boule is sliced on a diamond-wire saw, a production method pioneered by Switzerland's Meyer Burger. After grinding, polishing, and cleaning, the finished wafer is inspected for particles, defects, and microscopic surface variations using metrology tools from KLA (NASDAQ: KLAC). The tool supplier sits one step removed from the wafer itself, but it is essential: no fab wants to discover a substrate defect after adding expensive process layers.

→The payoffPurity was never the goal. Control is.

After all that purification, the fab deliberately contaminates the silicon. Boron makes p-type silicon — electron-hungry — while phosphorus makes n-type silicon — electron-rich. The junction between the two is the foundation a transistor is built from. So the fab's first act is choosing the wafer's dopant type: purity was never the goal, control is.

The economics are just as striking: the whole sand-to-wafer journey costs maybe a couple hundred dollars per wafer, while a single wafer's worth of AI GPUs can sell for millions. Essentially all the value is in what gets printed and assembled on top.

In plain English: doping is deliberate, controlled contamination — it creates the electrical push and pull that lets a transistor switch.

Deep dive: the wafer makers

The silicon wafer business is roughly a $12–13B-a-year market — small next to the ~$600B chip industry, but literally nothing gets made without it. And it's one of the purest oligopolies in tech: five companies control about 90% of supply.

Shin-Etsu Chemical (TSE: 4063), ~30% — the undisputed #1

A wonderfully weird company: simultaneously the world's largest wafer maker and the world's largest PVC (plastic pipe) producer. Founded in 1926 as a fertilizer company, now a chemistry-at-scale machine. Its semiconductor arm posts operating margins that can top 35%, among the best in the industry. Famously disciplined: mountain of net cash, basically no debt, invests counter-cyclically while rivals freeze in downturns. Also makes photoresists and photomask blanks, so it's embedded in the fab at multiple layers. The quality compounder of the group.

SUMCO (TSE: 3436), ~24% — the pure play

Born in 1999 from the merger of Sumitomo's and Mitsubishi's silicon divisions. Unlike Shin-Etsu, wafers are basically all it does — the cleanest read on wafer demand, but more cyclical: thinner margins, bigger swings.

GlobalWafers (TWO: 6488), ~15% — Taiwan's champion

Grew by acquisition (notably SunEdison Semiconductor in 2016). Its claim to fame: the failed €4.4B takeover of Siltronic in 2021–22, which collapsed when Germany's foreign-investment review never cleared it in time. Textbook proof governments now treat wafer supply as strategic infrastructure.

Siltronic (Xetra: WAF), ~11% — Germany's specialist

IPO'd in 2015 with Wacker Chemie as anchor shareholder — and Wacker is itself a top polysilicon producer, so Siltronic has unusual vertical integration from poly to polished wafer.

SK Siltron, ~9% — Korea's entry, private

SK Group bought it from LG in 2017, then grabbed DuPont's wafer business in 2020 (~$450M), gaining US capacity plus silicon carbide wafers — a different material for EV power electronics, not logic chips. No ticker.

Why only five survive — the moat

(1) Qualification: fabs take 12–24 months to approve a supplier, since one bad lot can scrap millions in processed chips — nobody switches to save a few percent. (2) Defect black art: decades of know-how in controlling crystal defects. (3) Scale: a new 300mm plant costs billions; incumbents expand in lockstep with customers via long-term agreements, often prepaid. Dozens of players in the 1990s consolidated into these five.

China's push

TCL Zhonghuan and a handful of domestic players are scaling 300mm capacity, but they're still mostly qualified for trailing-edge nodes — leading-edge wafers remain a Japan/Taiwan/Korea/Germany game.

The economics in one line

Oligopoly plus that qualification moat means real pricing power — leaders earn gross margins in the high 30s to 40s. Demand tracks fab utilization (logic + memory), smoothed by long-term agreements. Cyclical, but far less violent than memory chips.

Reference

Glossary

The terms that make the semiconductor chain legible.

Metallurgical-grade silicon (MG-Si)

Silicon that's 98–99% pure, made by melting quartz with carbon in a giant electric arc furnace. “Metallurgical” just means it's good enough for metal-making — it's mostly mixed into aluminum alloys. But for chips it's still far too dirty. Think of it as crude oil before refining.

Siemens process

The refining method that turns crude silicon into ultra-pure polysilicon. The silicon is converted into a gas (trichlorosilane), the gas is distilled to remove impurities — like distilling vodka, but for atoms — then baked onto white-hot rods where it turns back into solid silicon, now 99.9999999% pure. Developed by Siemens in the 1950s.

Trichlorosilane

A gas made of silicon, hydrogen, and chlorine. It's just a convenient vehicle: silicon is far easier to purify as a gas (distillation separates impurities beautifully) than as a solid. After purification it's converted back into solid silicon.

Polysilicon

Ultra-pure silicon in “many-crystal” form — millions of tiny crystals jumbled together (poly = many). It's the raw material that gets melted down for the crystal-pulling step.

Czochralski process

Pronounced “choh-KRAHL-skee.” The method for growing one giant single crystal from molten silicon. A small seed crystal is dipped into the melt and slowly pulled upward while rotating; the liquid freezes onto the seed in perfect alignment, growing into a crystal cylinder meters long.

Boule / ingot

The finished single-crystal cylinder — basically a giant silicon carrot, up to 2 meters long and 30 cm thick, weighing hundreds of kilos. “Boule” is French for a rounded lump; “ingot” is the generic word for a cast block of material.

Grain boundaries

The borders between individual crystals in a multi-crystal material. Each grain has its atoms aligned differently, and the mismatch at the boundary disrupts electrical behavior — which is exactly why chips demand a single crystal with zero grain boundaries.

Wafer

A thin slice of the ingot, about 0.7 mm thick, polished until its surface is flat at the atomic scale. This mirror-smooth disc is the blank canvas that goes into the fab.

Doping

Deliberately adding tiny amounts of other elements (usually boron or phosphorus — just a few atoms per billion) to change how the silicon conducts electricity. It creates the two “flavors” of silicon (p-type and n-type) that make transistors possible. Counterintuitive but essential: after all that purification, controlled contamination is the whole game.

9N purity

Shorthand for 99.9999999% pure — count the nines, there are nine of them. Fewer than one impurity atom per billion silicon atoms. (11N = eleven nines, even purer.)

Crucible

The container holding the molten silicon — itself made of ultra-pure quartz, because anything less pure would contaminate the melt.

Seed crystal

A small piece of perfect single-crystal silicon dipped into the melt to start growth. The melt's atoms lock onto the seed's pattern, so the entire ingot inherits its perfection.

Diamond-wire saw

The tool that slices the ingot into wafers — a wire coated with diamond dust, since diamond is about the only thing hard enough to cut silicon cleanly.

LTA (long-term agreement)

Multi-year supply contracts between wafer makers and chipmakers, often with the chipmaker prepaying to fund new capacity. They smooth out the industry's boom-bust cycle.

Wafer qualification

The 12–24 month testing gauntlet a chipmaker puts a new wafer supplier through before trusting their wafers in production. One bad batch can scrap millions of dollars of chips — this is the industry's stickiest moat.
